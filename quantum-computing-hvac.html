Quantum Computing & The HVAC Industry

BY MARIO MENDOZA, P.E., BCXP, HFDP, MEMBER ASHRAE; YELISSA LOPEZ; RONALD DIAZ, P.E., MEMBER ASHRAE; ADRIAN JOHANNES MENSSEN, PH.D.

The mechanical and energy-engineering field has witnessed successive waves of technological disruption—from variable refrigerant flow systems to digital twins and AI-based analytics. Quantum computing represents the next such disruption, one with direct implications for HVAC engineers and facility designers. Collaboration between the physics and engineering communities, including ongoing dialogue with researchers at a quantum computer company and MIT’s Photonics Laboratory, has revealed a significant gap—little to no publicly available guidance exists on designing mechanical systems for quantum computing environments. As firms and governments increase investment in the quantum sector, MEP engineers will play a vital role in developing robust, stable and energy-efficient infrastructures. The purpose of this article is to initiate that dialogue—bridging the gap between physics laboratories and mechanical design practice—and to invite ASHRAE’s technical community to establish design standards for this transformative field.

Environmental Requirements for Quantum Computing Systems

Quantum computers differ fundamentally from classical systems. Classical bits represent 0 or 1, can exist as a superposition—a linear combination—of both basis states simultaneously. Pairs of qubits can also become entangled, a phenomenon producing correlated measurement outcomes regardless of physical separation. Sustaining these states depends on quantum coherence, the preservation of the well-defined phase relationships among a qubit’s quantum states, which is what allows superposition and entanglement to be harnessed for computation. These properties underpin the theoretical advantages of quantum computing but also introduce extreme environmental sensitivities.

Qubits lose coherence through decoherence, which is the disruption of quantum states caused by environmental noise, including temperature fluctuations, magnetic fields, vibration and stray electromagnetic radiation. Independent technical reviews confirm that minimizing thermal fluctuations, electromagnetic interference and mechanical vibration is crucial for preserving qubit coherence and minimizing computational errors.1,2 Recent work has demonstrated that mechanical disturbances can introduce correlated errors across superconducting circuits.3

Four leading qubit architectures are currently being developed at commercial scale: superconducting, trapped-ion, neutral/cold atom and photonic. Each imposes distinct facility requirements on temperature, vibration, electromagnetic environment, humidity, particulates and power quality. Table 1 summarizes these requirements alongside relevant infrastructure considerations. Where quantitative tolerances have been established through published research or reported operational practice, they are noted; where formal standards do not yet exist, the values represent current guideline-level benchmarks only. Temperatures in this article are given in kelvin (K), the SI unit of absolute temperature, where 0 K is absolute zero (−273.15°C) and one millikelvin (mK) is one-thousandth of a kelvin.

Superconducting and trapped-ion systems represent the most technologically mature quantum-computing platforms, as evidenced by the scale of commercial investment and the public profile of their leading developers. Superconducting qubits are the architecture of choice for IBM and Google Quantum AI. Trapped-ion systems are advanced commercially by IonQ and Quantinuum. Despite this commercial maturity, their thermal requirements differ substantially. Superconducting qubits must be maintained at ultralow temperatures, typically around 10 mK, within dilution refrigerators to sustain superconductivity and suppress decoherence. In contrast, trapped-ion platforms generally operate near room temperature, relying on laser cooling and electromagnetic confinement of ions in ultra-high-vacuum chambers rather than cryogenic refrigeration.

Photonic quantum architectures use qubits that function at ambient temperature, but cryogenic cooling (≈ 4 K) is often required for superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors and associated optical-switching electronics that enable readout and entanglement distribution.

Across all leading qubit modalities, the preservation of coherence depends on maintaining exceptionally stable thermal, vibrational and electromagnetic environments, emphasizing the need for integrated HVAC and infrastructure strategies tailored to quantum-computing facilities.

Parallels with High-Tech Laboratory Design

Conversations with one of the lead researchers at MIT’s Photonics Laboratory revealed that current quantum setups more closely resemble advanced laser or nanotechnology laboratories than conventional data centers. Common design targets include:

  • Temperature stability. ±0.1°C (0.2°F) in enclosures; ±1°C (1.8°F) at the room level.
  • Humidity control. ≤50% RH to minimize optical-component drift.
  • Particulate control. HEPA or ULPA filtration with laminar diffusers.
  • Vibration isolation. Structural damping and decoupled mechanical systems.
  • Electromagnetic noise. Use of non-magnetic materials and implementation of well-planned grounding (e.g., star grounding to avoid ground loops) to minimize interference. Equipment should also be located away from sources of electromagnetic radiation such as microwaves, strong magnets and other radiating electrical devices.
  • Cooling infrastructure. Dedicated chilled-water or heat-exchanger loops with redundancy (~20 kW per rack).
  • Power quality. Independent, filtered circuits with harmonic suppression.

While these parameters resemble semiconductor cleanrooms, quantum laboratories demand tighter tolerances—often requiring thermal variation control of an order of magnitude smaller than standard data center thresholds.

Energy and Sustainability Considerations

Cryogenic systems and laser-stabilization units are energy-intensive. On a per-qubit basis, energy consumption can exceed that of conventional high-performance computing nodes. Near-term facility design must therefore account for substantial cooling loads, high-quality power distribution and the associated water and energy use intensity.

Longer-term, quantum computing is expected to accelerate discovery in sustainable materials relevant to the built environment. As an illustrative example: the cement and steel industries face a persistent challenge in reducing embodied carbon while meeting structural performance requirements. Quantum algorithms capable of modeling molecular interactions may enable the development of novel concretes and composites with lower embodied carbon and superior strength properties—a goal that classical computing cannot efficiently address due to the combinatorial complexity of quantum-mechanical simulations. Quantum-enabled breakthroughs in materials science could reduce global CO₂ emissions from cement, steel and building operations by more than 10%.8

Research at the University of Notre Dame has demonstrated quantum-enhanced design tools applied to “smart glass” materials that dynamically control solar heat gain, illustrating direct relevance to HVAC energy reduction. While such outcomes remain developmental, they suggest that the near-term energy burden of quantum facility infrastructure may ultimately be offset by the optimization capabilities these systems unlock.

Implications for the Construction and HVAC Industries

Design and Construction Outlook

As quantum computing transitions from laboratory to production scale, quantum data centers will become a distinct facility type. Mechanical engineers will need to synthesize design knowledge from ASHRAE TC 9.9 (Data Centers), TC 9.10 (Laboratories), and TC 9.11 (Clean Spaces). Key priorities include:

  • Ultra-stable micro-environments with advanced zoning and precise reheat control.
  • Modular, redundant systems designed for phased expansion.
  • Structural and material solutions that reduce vibration and magnetic interference.
  • Integrated controls with real-time monitoring of temperature, humidity and qubit-coherence parameters.

Digital Tools, Sensing Devices and Simulation

Beyond their facility requirements, quantum computing platforms may also transform HVAC engineering practice itself. Quantum algorithms have demonstrated advantages over classical solvers on certain optimization and fluid-dynamics problems13,14—the same classes of challenges encountered in building-energy modeling and computational fluid dynamics (CFD)-based airflow design. As algorithm maturity and qubit counts increase, such tools may become accessible to building simulation workflows.

Quantum sensing represents a parallel development of direct relevance to building systems. Quantum sensors exploit superposition and entanglement to achieve measurement sensitivities beyond the limits of classical instrumentation. Published examples include atomic interferometers for inertial sensing,15 optical clocks with sub-10−18 fractional frequency uncertainty16 and nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center magnetometers for sub-picotesla field detection. These capabilities come with trade-offs including cost, calibration complexity, cryogenic requirements for some modalities and limited operational robustness outside laboratory conditions. As these trade-offs are addressed, quantum sensors may enable new tiers of precision in building automation, system diagnostics and spatial positioning applications.

The Human and Economic Dimension

Beyond physics and engineering, workforce development will determine how fast the industry adapts. The demand for cross-disciplinary professionals (mechanical engineers versed in cryogenics, physicists familiar with HVAC systems and software developers attuned to real-time environmental data) is not yet reflected in standard professional development curricula.

Global public and private investment in quantum technology now exceeds $55 billion, reflecting rapid acceleration in national programs across the U.S., European Union, China, Japan and Canada, as well as major private initiatives led by IBM, Google, Amazon Web Services and PsiQuantum.9 Each new quantum research test bed, commercial facility or data center prototype represents a critical opportunity for the HVAC and building-engineering sectors to innovate—developing infrastructure capable of meeting the precision environmental requirements that underpin this emerging industry.

Toward a Framework for Quantum-Facility Design

To ensure readiness and consistency, ASHRAE and allied organizations could:

  1. Establish a Technical Committee on quantum-facility environmental design to consolidate expertise and coordinate with relevant existing TCs (9.9, 9.10, 9.11).
  2. Develop addenda to existing laboratory and data center guidelines addressing vibration, electromagnetic interference (EMI) and cryogenic integration, using existing quantum facilities as data sources.
  3. Facilitate collaboration between physicists and building engineers to better understand and define environmental tolerances for each kind of qubit technology.
  4. Engage standards bodies such as ISO, NIST and semiconductor-industry consortia to align quantum-facility design criteria with adjacent high-tech laboratory standards where applicable.

As noted in industry analyses, early engagement by the engineering community will accelerate maturity and reliability within the quantum ecosystem, helping shape the standards that will govern future development.

Conclusion

Quantum computing represents both a design challenge and a transformative opportunity for the HVAC and construction industries. Each qubit architecture—superconducting, trapped-ion, photonic or neutral-atom—requires a unique environmental strategy, yet no formal design standards currently exist.

Developing clear, technology-specific HVAC guidelines is essential to ensure operational stability, safety and scalability. At the same time, quantum computing’s own optimization capabilities promise to revolutionize energy modeling and sustainable building design.

By engaging early, ASHRAE and its members can help define the environmental parameters for quantum infrastructure while positioning the HVAC profession at the forefront of this next technological frontier.
